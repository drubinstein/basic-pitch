How to use

Everything runs in your browser. Audio never leaves your machine.

Open a file

Click the search bar at the top left (“Open or drop an audio file…”), drop a file anywhere on the page, or press Try an example (vocadito track 10, a sung melody). WAV, MP3, AAC, FLAC and Ogg work, depending on what your browser can decode.

Live or Original

Live makes notes with a streaming decoder (a hidden Markov model, see the report) while the model runs, so notes appear as soon as each one is final. Original uses Basic Pitch's own note creation, exactly as the basic-pitch package does it with its default settings. That method finds onset peaks across the file and then uses the melodia trick: it repeatedly takes the strongest leftover activation anywhere in the file and traces it forwards and backwards in time into a note (“Posteriorgram post-processing”, section 3 of the Basic Pitch paper; the name comes from Melodia, a melody extractor that builds pitch contours the same way; code). It needs the whole file, so its notes come at the end. Switching modes keeps the music playing.

Notes

Hover a note for its name, MIDI number, frequency and timing. Click to pin it; esc or a click elsewhere clears it. In Live mode the tooltip also shows the decoder lag: how long after the note began the decoder was sure of its start.

Save

The download button in the search bar writes the notes the way basic-pitch does (120 bpm, Electric Piano 1, velocity from how strong the note is), without pitch bends. Finished results are kept in this browser, per file and mode, so reopening a file is instant; the circular arrow next to it transcribes again. Saved results: .

Streaming Basic Pitch: what we tried, and why

How we got notes out of Basic Pitch while the audio comes in, what the numbers said, and why the site works the way it does. November 2026.

The problem

The Basic Pitch model already runs window by window, so it can stream. The note creation step after it can't: it finds onset peaks across the file, and the melodia trick repeatedly takes the strongest leftover activation anywhere in the file. So notes only exist once the whole file is in. The goal was notes as the audio arrives, without retraining Basic Pitch.

From a traceback length to an HMM

We started by treating note creation like a Viterbi decoder with a fixed traceback length, the way streaming decoders for error-correcting codes work: keep the best explanation of the audio so far for every possible current state, and once the explanations agree about a frame, or after a fixed delay at the latest, commit to it. A short look through the literature showed that this generalizes to a hidden Markov model decoded with the Viterbi algorithm, run online, where a decision is final as soon as all surviving paths merge (Šrámek et al.) and the traceback length becomes a cap on how long a decision can wait. HMMs with attack and sustain states are also an established way to turn frame-by-frame pitch estimates into notes (Ryynänen & Klapuri, Poliner & Ellis).

What an HMM is

A hidden Markov model describes something you can't observe directly as a chain of states. Here, for each of the 88 piano pitches, the hidden state in each frame (11.6 ms) says whether that pitch is off, in the first few frames of a note (attack), held (sustain), or in a short dip inside a note (gap). The model says two things: how likely each move from one state to the next is, and how well each state explains what Basic Pitch reports for that frame (its note and onset activations). The Viterbi algorithm finds the single most likely sequence of states; each run from attack through sustain is a note.

The chain is what makes it better than a threshold: a flicker in the activation can't make a new note unless an onset (or very strong evidence) starts one, and a short dip inside a held note stays one note.

Running it as the audio arrives: at each new frame, Viterbi keeps one best path into each state. Followed back, those paths usually join within a few frames; everything before the join can't change any more, so it's final. That is the decoder lag shown in the note tooltips. If they haven't joined after 100 frames (1.16 s), the decoder commits to the best path anyway. That cap is the original traceback length.

What we tried

  • A fair baseline. The original note creation was tuned too (per dataset, on the dev halves). Tuned, it gains a lot: onset threshold 0.625, frame threshold 0.3, minimum length 7, energy tolerance 4, and the melodia trick off.
  • Tuning the HMM. Hill climbing gave all the gains. A PufferLib Protein sweep with latency as the cost never beat the starting parameters at the same latency. Per-register parameters didn't carry over from dev to test, and stronger re-strike handling made things worse.
  • A learned decoder (an RNN, below).
  • A ceiling check. The HMM tuned directly on the vocadito test tracks, to see how good any HMM tuning could get.

The RNN option

Why it was attractive. The HMM looks at each pitch on its own, so it can't use what the other pitches are doing: the octave above, the rest of a chord, harmonics, or a string that's still ringing when it's struck again. A small recurrent network reading all 88 pitches' note and onset activations can, and a few frames of look-ahead (a fixed delay, like the traceback length) lets it see a note's attack before committing.

What we built. Per-pitch convolutions plus a 128-unit GRU across pitches, trained on a held-out split of GuitarSet, MAESTRO and vocadito for about 30 minutes on a CPU, with 0, 4 or 8 frames (0, 46 or 93 ms) of look-ahead, then its decoding thresholds tuned on held-out tracks.

What happened. It lost to the tuned original on most tracks. With 4 frames of look-ahead it beat it on 46% of GuitarSet tracks (Fno) but on 9% of MAESTRO and 1 of 20 vocadito tracks (vocadito Fno .475 against .559). No look-ahead and 8 frames did worse. Half an hour on a CPU is far too little training for a model like this.

Why we stopped there. The ceiling check says even an HMM tuned on the very tracks it's scored on beats the tuned original on only 80% (Fno) and 85% (F) of vocadito tracks. A learned decoder could only do better by fixing errors the HMM structurally can't, the cross-pitch ones, and we don't yet know that those make up most of what's left. Different decoders reading the same model outputs land at about the same score, which points at the model's outputs rather than the decoding. A real attempt needs a GPU run of hours, more held-out labelled audio (for example Slakh2100's test split), a training loss closer to note-level F, and the same track-by-track check.

Why we went with what we did

  • The streaming HMM is the Live mode. It makes notes while the audio comes in, typically within a few frames of their start. It is far better than the original's defaults and close to the tuned original: ahead on vocadito, behind on MAESTRO Fno (.748 vs .774) and GuitarSet F (.600 vs .636, mostly re-strikes of ringing strings). Tuned per dataset, it pulls ahead on MAESTRO too.
  • It still uses its starting parameters. The tuned sets are per dataset, add about 100 ms, and gain 0.01 to 0.04 Fno. There is no single tuned set yet that's better everywhere.
  • No learned decoder yet, for the reasons above.
  • Original mode stays for comparison. It is a line-by-line port of output_to_notes_polyphonic with predict()'s defaults, melodia trick included, and gives the same notes as the Python code on the test fixtures. Its MIDI files match basic-pitch's notes and velocities.
  • The site works like a map. Whatever is on screen is transcribed first. Each run of windows starts one window early so its notes match decoding from the start, the decoder keeps only 100 frames of history, and finished results are cached in the browser. On a 10-minute file, jumping around gave exactly the same 1,759 notes as going front to back, at about 97× real time on 4 threads.

Future work

  • Train the learned decoder properly: on a GPU, with more held-out labelled audio, judged by the same track-by-track check. It's the one option here that can fix cross-pitch errors.
  • One tuned HMM parameter set that works across instruments, instead of one per dataset.
  • Ceiling checks on GuitarSet and MAESTRO (each guitar trial takes 20–120 s).
  • Slakh2100, for multi-instrument music: stream only the test mixes and their MIDI out of the 104 GB archive instead of downloading all of it.
  • Color-coded instrument tracks. Basic Pitch doesn't know which instrument played a note. Separating the audio into stems first (for example with Hybrid Transformer Demucs: vocals, bass, drums, other) and transcribing each would give one color per track, at the cost of a much larger download and several times the compute.
  • Microphone input, for streaming end to end.
  • Pitch bends in the MIDI, from the model's contour output.
  • Safari and Firefox testing.
  • Better model outputs. That's where the remaining errors seem to be, but it means retraining Basic Pitch, which was out of scope.

About

Basic Pitch Live runs Spotify's Basic Pitch transcription model in your browser with LiteRT.js, and turns its output into notes with a streaming decoder while it runs. Audio never leaves your machine; finished results are saved in this browser only.
